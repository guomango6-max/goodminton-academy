双打里常有人问：“到底哪一拍最值得练？”如果只能给一个答案，我不会选杀球，也不会选某一种接发技术。我会选发球、接发和第三拍之间的连接。

Badminton Bible 在双打发球战术中把发球目标说得很直接：争取本方先获得进攻机会。好发球不一定直接得分。它更常见的作用，是让接发方只能挑球，或者只能交出质量不高的网前球。它还解释了为什么直线低发球常被当作基础选择：飞行距离短，对手反应时间少，回球角度也更受限制。偶尔的偷后场则用于阻止对手把全部准备都押在低发球上。

这段来源直接支持的是发球选择。接下来关于主动权、红黄绿状态和三拍衔接的部分，是 Goodminton 的训练框架。

在 Goodminton，主动权不是“我正在进攻”，而是三件事同时成立：我有能执行的选择，对方的选择被压缩，而且我打完以后还能准备下一拍。用这个标准看，前三拍各自有不同任务。

第一拍，发球者不是在追求一拍赢球，而是在管理接发方的选择。直线低发球如果贴网、落点稳定，可以让对手更难直接制造大角度；偷后场只有在动作准备相似、长度足够时才是有效变化。质量不够的“变化”不会增加选择，反而把主动交出去。

第二拍，接发者先判断自己是什么颜色。来球高、身体平衡、击球点在身前，可以看作绿态：有资格推空档、压中腰或抢网。球点一般、双方都还没有明显优势，是黄态：选择要稳，目标是让下一拍比这一拍更主动。来球已经低、身体被挤住或只能迟点击球，就是红态：这时不要假装自己还能强攻。把球挑深、挡到对方难连续压迫的位置，先恢复时间和空间，比硬抢一拍更合理。

第三拍决定前两拍有没有形成链条。发球方如果逼出了质量不高的接发，前场球员要准备封直线和中路，后场搭档要准备接管被挑起的球。接发方如果成功压住发球，搭档也不能站着看结果，而要根据最短时间内可能造成伤害的回球，先完成保护。第三拍之后，双方都要重新回答同一个问题：现在谁的选择更多，我下一拍准备在哪里？

训练前三拍，不要一上来就打完整比赛。先把责任关系练清楚，再增加变化。

- 第一层：固定低发球，接发者只回两个指定区域。发球者练发完后的第一步，搭档练谁封网、谁接后场。
- 第二层：接发者可以推或挑，但必须在击球后说出自己是红、黄还是绿。判断错了不急着纠动作，先看他为什么误判了时间和空间。
- 第三层：只打前三拍，第四拍停球。双方不按前三拍是否“赢球”计分，而按第四拍开始时谁拥有更多真实选择计分。
- 第四层：进入正常回合，取消口令。课后再检查固定练习里的选择能不能迁移到不同发球质量、不同站位和真实压力下。

这种计分方式会揭开一个常见假象：训练中某一拍打得很漂亮，不代表前三拍已经学会。如果离开固定喂球就不知道站哪里，或者每次都要等教练提醒才准备下一拍，那只是当场表现，不是稳定能力。

Players often ask which single shot matters most in doubles. If I had to choose, I would not pick the smash or one particular return. I would choose the connection between the serve, the return, and the third shot.

Badminton Bible describes the server’s objective in simple terms: create the first attacking opportunity for your side. A good serve does not need to win the rally outright. More often, it makes the receiver lift or play a weak net reply. The same guide explains why the straight low serve is such a useful base option. It travels a short distance, gives the receiver less time, and limits the available angles. An occasional flick serve prevents the receiver from committing completely to the low serve.

That source supports the serving choices. The initiative model, traffic-light states, and practice design below come from the Goodminton coaching framework.

At Goodminton, initiative does not simply mean “I am attacking.” It means that I have executable choices, my opponent’s choices are being reduced, and I can still prepare for the next shot. Each of the first three shots has a different job within that definition.

On the first shot, the server manages the receiver’s options rather than hunting for an ace. A straight low serve that stays tight and lands consistently can make immediate angles harder to create. A flick is useful only when the preparation looks similar and the serve reaches a safe height and depth. A low-quality variation does not create uncertainty. It gives initiative away.

On the second shot, the receiver should first read the current state. A high contact in front of a balanced body is green: a push into space, pressure through the middle, or a committed net interception may be available. A neutral contact with no clear advantage is yellow: the return should improve the next situation without taking an unnecessary risk. A late, low contact while crowded or off balance is red. In red, forcing an attack is usually false ambition. A deep lift or a controlled reply that interrupts the opponent’s next pressure may be the better way to recover time and space.

The third shot reveals whether the first two shots formed a chain. When the serving side produces a loose return, the front player should be ready to close the straight channel and the middle while the rear partner prepares for the lift. When the receiving side gains pressure, the receiver’s partner cannot wait to see where the shuttle goes. That player must first protect the reply that can do irreversible damage in the shortest time. After the third shot, both pairs face the same question: who now has more real choices, and where should I prepare for the next shot?

Do not begin first-three-shot training with unrestricted games. Clarify responsibilities first, then add uncertainty.

- Layer one: use a fixed low serve and allow returns to only two target areas. The server practises the first movement after serving, while the partner learns who closes the net and who takes the rear court.
- Layer two: allow either a push or a lift. The receiver must call red, yellow, or green after contact. If the call is wrong, examine the time-and-space judgement before correcting the stroke.
- Layer three: stop after the third shot. Do not score only by who “won” those shots. Score which side has more executable choices when the fourth shot would begin.
- Layer four: return to a normal rally and remove the verbal cues. Check later whether the choices survive different serve quality, changed positions, and real pressure.

This scoring exposes a common illusion. One impressive shot during a fixed drill does not prove that the first three shots have been learned. If a player loses position when the feed changes, or needs the coach to announce every next movement, the drill has produced temporary performance rather than a stable match skill.
